## The chain in one paragraph

The Introduction opens with two puzzles. The theoretical one: how
environmental concerns interact with social well-being, once well-being is
taken as a policy domain with institutions of its own and not only as
income. The empirical one: how Amazon deforestation fell between 2004 and
2012 while the region grew and social inclusion expanded, and why the
trend reversed. The two meet at the frontier, where well-being and
conservation do not align by default and where the institutional
configuration the eco-social literature assumes does not hold. Chapter 1 reviews the traditions in environmental politics and policy
(economic, political economy, and institutional lenses, in that order),
shows that they treat environmental and social policy as separate
domains, presents the Eco-Social State literature as the integrating
move, defends the eco-social perspective on the review's three threads
and gives it distributive content through the asset typology (rent and
livelihood holders of degradation-forcing and forest-dependent assets),
identifies its two gaps (no "worlds of the eco-social state" typology;
near-exclusive focus on advanced capitalist democracies), and derives a
framework of three operationalizable dimensions (distributive conflict,
institutional configuration, multilevel governance) for frontier
settings. Chapter 2 demonstrates
historically that these dimensions are constitutive of Amazonian
environmental governance, develops the two-arenas contrast and the
eco-social arena concept with its two mechanisms, and justifies the period
of interest. Chapter 3 conducts a PRISMA systematic review of the last
~10 years of causal research on the causes of Amazon deforestation
(quantitative and qualitative causal claims), maps the causes through the
Chapter 1 lenses, and formalizes the literature's theoretical assumptions
as DAGs — the state of the art on causal inference for this object.
Chapter 4 then tests, descriptively and causally, whether and under what
conditions social policy shapes deforestation directly or by conditioning
the effectiveness of environmental enforcement, positioned against the
gaps Chapter 3 identifies.

## The introduction's framing (produced there, absorbed by every chapter)

- **Two puzzles and a hinge.** The theoretical puzzle (well-being as a
  policy domain in the environmental debate) and the Amazon puzzle (the
  decline of 2004-2012 and its reversal), joined at the frontier. The
  theoretical puzzle has two layers (recorded 2026-10-18): the basic one,
  how environmental issues interact with economic growth and well-being,
  and the specific one, how environmental action interacts with
  well-being institutions. The specific layer is what the introduction
  calls the institutional layer, and Chapter 1 answers it.
- **Two kinds of dependence** (added 2026-10-17; absorbed by Chapter 1's
  guidance on 2026-10-18 in asset terms, see the end of this item; Ch2
  and Ch4 pending). Livelihood dependence: households and local
  economies live from a degrading activity. Institutional dependence: the
  state's protective institutions are financed and legitimated by the
  growth that activity feeds. In Europe the two coincide (growth-dependent
  welfare), so environmental policy threatens livelihoods and the welfare
  state's own base at once. On the frontier, livelihood dependence is high
  and institutional dependence is low: the protection that reached it is
  non-contributory and federally financed, and municipal budgets rest on
  federal transfers (Ch4's revenue evidence). This gives mechanism A a
  structural possibility Europe lacks, while the separation of arenas lets
  mechanism B operate by default. Which prevails is Ch4's question; the
  introduction poses it and does not answer it. Institutional dependence
  varies by level of government (states on ICMS from agriculture and
  cattle, municipalities on transfers), which is the two-arenas contrast
  restated in fiscal terms. Chapter 1 restates the distinction through
  its asset typology: livelihood dependence is holding a
  degradation-forcing livelihood asset with no exit; institutional
  dependence is the state itself holding degradation-forcing assets.

## The three operationalizable dimensions (derived in Ch1, applied in Ch2, operationalized in Ch4)

Revised 2026-10-18 (dimension 2 was "social conditions of state action").
The three are partitioned by the kind of question each asks: who gains
and who pays (1); what rules and provisions exist (2); how rules are made
and applied across levels of government (3). The line between 2 and 3 is
the line between a rule and its enforcement.

1. **Distributive conflict.** Who captures the rents of degradation and
   who bears the costs of conservation on the frontier, and how the
   conflict is politically organized. Operationalized through Chapter 1's
   asset typology: who holds degradation-forcing and forest-dependent
   assets, as rent or as livelihood (the livelihood row covers the whole
   population whose well-being depends, directly or indirectly, on the
   degrading activity).
2. **Institutional configuration.** What the state does, and does not do,
   as the determinant of how individuals and communities respond to
   environmental action: market incentives and regulation, the labor
   regime and welfare provision (with the fiscal base it rests on), and
   conservation measures. Carries the introduction's question of which
   configuration holds on the frontier.
3. **Multilevel governance.** The institutional and political arrangements
   through which environmental policy is made and enforced across levels
   of government: the behavior of political actors and the subnational
   variation in enforcement.

## Chapter 2's central constructs (produced there, consumed downstream)

- **Two-phase macro-arc.** Phase 1: state-led environmental exploitation
  with regulated exclusion of frontier labor (c. 1870–1985). Phase 2:
  environmental protection and social inclusion built simultaneously from
  the 1988 critical juncture, in separate arenas that never integrated
  (1988–2022).
- **Two-arenas contrast.** Both arenas are federally regulated; the contrast
  is the territorial distribution of costs and benefits. Social policy:
  central regulation plus aligned local incentives → territorial
  convergence. Environmental enforcement: central regulation imposing
  concentrated local costs with dispersed benefits → territorial
  unevenness and contestation.
- **Eco-social arena, mechanisms A and B.** A — cushioning as compensation:
  social protection absorbs the costs of regulation, creates local stakes
  in conservation, strengthens enforcement. B — cushioning as forbearance:
  social vulnerability licenses non-enforcement, weakening the
  environmental state. Historical claim: without integration, A remained
  marginal and B operated by default.
- **Asymmetric resilience** (2016–2022) as the observable implication and
  hypothesis test: environmental apparatus drifted; the social track,
  defended by mass constituencies, was structurally preserved.

## Chapter 3's deliverables (the state of the art)

- A PRISMA-documented corpus of the last ~10 years of causal research on
  Amazon deforestation (window adjustable per initial results; causal
  claims only, quantitative or qualitative).
- A typology of the causes identified in that literature, classified
  through the Chapter 1 lenses.
- An inventory of designs and identification strategies already applied to
  this object.
- DAGs formalizing the theoretical assumptions of the reviewed literature,
  including conflicts between studies' implied causal structures.
- The gaps the dissertation addresses — in particular, whether and how
  social policy figures in this literature as a studied cause or condition.

## Chapter 4's hypotheses (the testable form of the chain)

- **H1.** Social conditions and social policy have a direct effect on
  deforestation; the comparative literature leaves the sign open.
- **H2.** Social-policy coverage moderates the effect of environmental
  enforcement (the eco-social synergy hypothesis; the empirical form of
  the A-versus-B question).
- **H3.** The moderation is itself conditioned by electoral and
  institutional context.

The leading operationalization of H2 is Bolsa Família coverage and social expenditures conditioning
the effect of PPCDAm-era enforcement on municipal deforestation, but the
operationalization is not fixed in advance: Chapter 4's guidance maintains a
map of candidate sources of identifying variation, and design choice follows
the structure of treatment variation — informed by Chapter 3's inventory of
what has already been tried — not a prior commitment.
