## Chapter's Purpose and Argument

Chapter 1 provides the dissertation's theoretical foundation by critically reviewing the main traditions in environmental politics and policy and assessing how they conceptualize the relationship between public policies, environmental outcomes, and social dimensions, particularly inequality and welfare provision. Such a narrative review matters because it helps us better evaluate current studies on the causes of Amazon rainforest deforestation. In other words, it allows us to spotlight the different assumptions or the underlying perspectives that are present in the scholarship, even when they are not made explicit by the authors. This helps show how the study enters the debate on Amazon degradation and, more broadly, what advances it brings to understanding environmental and climate issues.

The chapter organizes this literature around three primary analytical lenses (economic, political economy, and institutional), showing how they emphasize distinct causal mechanisms (individual behavior, wealth and power distribution, and the role of the state) and levels of analysis (micro, meso, and macro).

The chapter argues that, despite the differences, these traditions have largely treated environmental policy and social policy as analytically separate domains, giving limited attention to how welfare provision and labor structures shape environmental governance and policy effectiveness. It then examines the emerging Eco-Social State literature as a significant step toward integrating social and environmental policy domains, as it explicitly theorizes the interdependence between social protection and social indicators, and environmental protection and environmental outcomes.

The chapter further identifies two key analytical gaps in the Eco-Social State literature. First, it lacks a well-developed framework explaining how a country's institutional configurations shape the outcomes of interactions between social-environmental policies and indicators. In other words, the Eco-Social State literature doesn't yet have a well-developed "worlds of Eco-Social State" typology, as the welfare-state scholarship does. Second, in its attempts to develop such a framework, the Eco-Social State literature remains disproportionately focused on welfare-state transformation and green transitions in advanced capitalist democracies, with limited attention to frontier settings characterized by high social vulnerability, rapid environmental degradation, recent expansion of welfare provision, and uneven subnational enforcement of environmental policies.

In doing so, this study joins the ongoing task of explaining the nexus between environmental outcomes and eco-social policies, focusing precisely on contexts where policy effectiveness is most contingent on social and institutional conditions. The Brazilian Amazon, therefore, constitutes a critical case, as it concentrates these tensions in a particularly acute form: on the one hand, persistent environmental degradation marked by recent waves of increase and decrease in forest conservation; on the other hand, deep social inequality and local dependence on environmentally harmful activities impacted by recent nationwide welfare expansion; in both realms, highly uneven state presence across local territories.

## The importance of the narrative review

> Note (author, Sept 2026): this section will be toned down after the current revision round, with part of its content moving into the chapter draft itself.

In the social sciences, a clear account of a study's theoretical basis serves three purposes.

The first, central to studies that make causal claims, is that theory identifies and defines the variables that explain a phenomenon. The epistemological literature shows that scientists who study causes and effects without experimental manipulation must have a causal model in place before collecting and analyzing data. With experimental manipulation (a randomized controlled trial), the design itself answers the causal question because the researcher intervenes and observes the effect. But in the social sciences we usually conduct observational studies of past phenomena that cannot be reproduced in a laboratory by a simple intervention. Here data alone cannot answer our questions, because the answer depends on imagining other worlds and other courses of history. We are in the domain of counterfactuals. Testing a hypothesis in that domain requires assuming, in the first place, a set of relationships between variables that the data do not show by themselves [@pearl2018, p.34]. Theory supplies the causal models that must guide scientific observation.

As we will show, studies of policies in the Amazon rainforest draw on a varied set of assumptions to select the factors of interest and to shape their explanations. In doing so, they rely on a specific understanding given by theory, whether they make it explicit or not. Making that theory explicit has two consequences. It ensures that we work with falsifiable theories, as @popper2005(1934) requires, since we cannot test what we have not stated. And it is a form of what @bourdieu1991 call epistemological rupture. For these authors, following Bachelard, the scientific object is not given by the phenomenon itself. It is constructed against common-sense prenotions and against the unexamined assumptions that circulate in a field. Making explicit the theory that Amazon deforestation studies use implicitly is a step of this kind because we turn assumptions that pass as descriptions of the phenomenon into propositions that can be examined and tested. @king1994 [p.15] add that a research question should make a specific contribution to an identifiable scholarly literature, increasing our collective capacity to construct verified explanations of some aspect of the world. In their words, "no empirical investigation can be successful without theory to guide its choice of questions" [@king1994, p.28]. In this sense, our investigation is theoretically guided.

The second purpose follows from King, Keohane and Verba's other criterion for a research question: it should be "important in the real world" [-@king1994, p.15], that is, consequential for political, social or economic life, or for understanding and predicting events that harm or benefit many people. The theory that guides our understanding cannot be simply the one the analyst prefers. It must fit the reality under study and answer questions and problems found on the ground. @shapiro2002 makes this argument against what he calls method-driven and theory-driven research, in which scholars deduce problems from their theories and methods rather than from the world, apply their tools to whatever fits them, and produce artificial constructs that make little sense outside the discipline. Our theoretical object, environmental degradation, and our empirical object, Amazon deforestation, must therefore be approached through theories built to explain what leads to success in controlling degradation and how those causes interact with another central concern, the social one. In Shapiro's terms, our investigation is a problem-driven one.

The third purpose concerns the decision to bring new theories into this review and to argue that they fit reality better than established ones. This decision has costs and benefits. A common criterion holds that simpler explanations are preferable when they explain reality well. King, Keohane and Verba express one version of this as leverage: "explaining as much as possible with as little as possible" [-@king1994, p.29]. If a few causal variables accurately explain what at first appears to be a complicated effect, our leverage over the problem is high. Another version is parsimony, which rests on the postulate that "simple theories have higher prior probabilities" [JEFFREYS, 1961, p. 47, cited in @king1994, p.20], a claim close to Occam's razor. King, Keohane and Verba treat parsimony as an assumption about the nature of the world, which is taken to be simple, and for that reason decline to adopt it as a general rule. For the authors, some parts of the world are simple, and others are complex [-@king1994, p.20]. Complexity, in turn, is not a fixed property of the phenomenon. Social science tries to make sense of situations we perceive as more or less complex, and that perception depends on whether we can specify outcomes and explanatory variables coherently. As they put it, "complexity is partly conditional on the state of our theory" [@king1994, p.10]. More observations may help, but they rarely suffice.

Popper's account of testability dissolves part of this tension. For @popper2005(1934), a theory that says more about the world has greater empirical content, prohibits more possible states of affairs, and is for that reason more falsifiable. In his sense, the theory with more content is also the simpler one. What matters is not the number of variables but how much a theory forbids and how exposed it is to refutation. A proposition that engages more closely with how the phenomenon works is more falsifiable and, in this sense, more scientific. @lakatos1978 extends this reasoning from single theories to series of theories, or research programmes, and offers a criterion for preferring a new theory over an established one. A theory T2 supersedes T1 when it accounts for everything T1 explained, has excess empirical content, that is, predicts facts that T1 did not, and has some of that excess content corroborated. A shift of this kind is progressive. A shift that merely accommodates anomalies without adding content is degenerating. This criterion frames the task of the present review. The eco-social state literature is worth bringing into the study of Amazon deforestation only if it accounts for what economic, political-economy, and institutional explanations already account for, and adds testable content about the interdependence between social policy and environmental outcomes. Knowing the established theories and their rivals is what allows us to build explanations that are at once better fitted to reality and no more complex than they need to be.

## Chapter guidances

### The narrative arc

This review is a narrative literature review, a historically structured argument, not an annotated bibliography. It traces debates in this order:

1.  Tragedy of the Commons and collective action (Hardin, Olson)
2.  Governance of common-pool resources (Ostrom)
3.  Environmental economics / Environmental Kuznets Curve
4.  Environmental political economy / inequality-environment nexus (Boyce and lineage)
5.  The Environmental State (Duit, Meadowcroft)
6.  Sustainable Welfare State / Eco-Welfare State (Gough, Koch, Fritz, Zimmermann, Hirvilammi, Bohnenberger)

### Analytical threads

The review tracks cross-cutting analytical threads across each moment in the literature, such as:

- **Analytical Lens (Economic, Political Economy, Institutional):** How each approach explains environmental outcomes through different primary mechanisms, such as market dynamics and incentives (economic), distribution and power (political economy), or rules, governance, and state capacity (institutional).
- **Context of theory emergence:** see the corresponding item in the supervisor-review checklist above.
- **Level of Analysis (Micro, Meso/Multilevel, Macro):** Whether the approach locates the primary drivers of environmental outcomes at the level of individual and group behavior (micro), subnational and multilevel governance arrangements (meso), or broader structural, institutional, and state-level processes (macro), and how it conceptualizes interactions across these levels.
- **The Environmental–Social Relationship:** How each approach conceptualizes the relationship between environmental sustainability and social protection dimensions, such as inequality, welfare provision, and redistribution.

#### Current description of the argument

The Eco-Social State literature rests on two assumptions that do not hold in frontier settings: a consolidated welfare state and a state that enforces the environmental rules it adopts. Latin American scholarship on welfare expansion and on institutional weakness theorizes precisely the conditions under which these assumptions fail, and the chapter draws on it to extend the framework. The first body of work explains the recent incorporation of "outsiders," the population outside formal employment and therefore outside contributory social insurance. It attributes this incorporation to redemocratization, electoral competition, left-wing parties, and social movement pressure [@arretche2018; @garay2016]. The second body of work shows that non-enforcement of regulations, including environmental ones, is often a political choice rather than a capacity deficit. Politicians may deliberately forbear from enforcing rules whose application would impose costs on poor constituents, using non-enforcement as a form of informal redistribution [@holland2016]. Enforcement of forest protection, in turn, varies subnationally with the balance of power between landowner coalitions and social or environmental movements, and with the alignment between national and subnational governments [@milmanda2019; @milmanda2020a; @milmanda2020b].

#### The three operationalizable dimensions

Building on these insights, the chapter proposes a framework for analyzing the nexus between social and environmental policy in frontier contexts marked by recent welfare expansion and uneven enforcement. The framework has three operationalizable dimensions:

1.  **Distributive conflict around environment:** The local political economy of environmental exploitation/regulation, defined by who bears the costs of conservation and who captures the rents of degradation.
2.  **Social conditions of state action:** Inequality, labor structure, and welfare provision as determinants of how individuals and communities respond to state action in other policy realms.
3.  **Multilevel governance:** The institutional arrangements through which environmental policy is made and enforced across levels of government, and the resulting subnational variation in enforcement.
