## Chapter's Purpose and Argument

Chapter 1 provides the dissertation's theoretical foundation by critically reviewing the main traditions in environmental politics and policy and assessing how they conceptualize the relationship between public policies, environmental outcomes, and social dimensions, particularly inequality and welfare provision. Such a narrative review matters because it helps us better evaluate current studies on the causes of Amazon rainforest deforestation. In other words, it allows us to spotlight the different assumptions or the underlying perspectives that are present in the scholarship, even when they are not made explicit by the authors. This helps show how the study enters the debate on Amazon degradation and, more broadly, what advances it brings to understanding environmental and climate issues.

The chapter answers the specific form of the introduction's theoretical puzzle: how environmental action interacts with well-being institutions (the basic form, how environmental issues interact with economic growth and well-being, is the introduction's). It organizes the literature by analytical lens (economic, political economy, and institutional) and reads each tradition through three threads: the lens itself, which fixes the mechanism the tradition favors (income-seeking behavior, the distribution of wealth and power, the effects of institutions) and what it takes well-being to be; the level and range of its argument (aggregate outcomes under general mechanisms, or arrangements that vary by context); and the locus of institutions in its explanation.

The chapter argues that, despite the differences, these traditions have largely treated environmental policy and social policy as analytically separate domains, giving limited attention to how welfare provision and labor structures shape environmental governance and policy effectiveness. It then examines the emerging Eco-Social State literature as a significant step toward integrating social and environmental policy domains, as it explicitly theorizes the interdependence between social protection and social indicators, and environmental protection and environmental outcomes. Before turning to that literature's gaps, the chapter defends the eco-social perspective on the three threads and gives it the distributive content it lacks, borrowed from political economy through the asset typology (see "The defense of the eco-social perspective").

The chapter further identifies two key analytical gaps in the Eco-Social State literature. First, it lacks a well-developed framework explaining how a country's institutional configurations shape the outcomes of interactions between social-environmental policies and indicators. In other words, the Eco-Social State literature doesn't yet have a well-developed "worlds of Eco-Social State" typology, as the welfare-state scholarship does. Second, in its attempts to develop such a framework, the Eco-Social State literature remains disproportionately focused on welfare-state transformation and green transitions in advanced capitalist democracies, with limited attention to frontier settings characterized by high social vulnerability, rapid environmental degradation, recent expansion of welfare provision, and uneven subnational enforcement of environmental policies.

In doing so, this study joins the ongoing task of explaining the nexus between environmental outcomes and eco-social policies, focusing precisely on contexts where policy effectiveness is most contingent on social and institutional conditions. The Brazilian Amazon, therefore, constitutes a critical case, as it concentrates these tensions in a particularly acute form: on the one hand, persistent environmental degradation marked by recent waves of increase and decrease in forest conservation; on the other hand, deep social inequality and local dependence on environmentally harmful activities impacted by recent nationwide welfare expansion; in both realms, highly uneven state presence across local territories.

## The importance of the narrative review

> Note (author, Sept 2026): this section will be toned down after the current revision round, with part of its content moving into the chapter draft itself.

In the social sciences, a clear account of a study's theoretical basis serves three purposes.

The first, central to studies that make causal claims, is that theory identifies and defines the variables that explain a phenomenon. The epistemological literature shows that scientists who study causes and effects without experimental manipulation must have a causal model in place before collecting and analyzing data. With experimental manipulation (a randomized controlled trial), the design itself answers the causal question because the researcher intervenes and observes the effect. But in the social sciences we usually conduct observational studies of past phenomena that cannot be reproduced in a laboratory by a simple intervention. Here data alone cannot answer our questions, because the answer depends on imagining other worlds and other courses of history. We are in the domain of counterfactuals. Testing a hypothesis in that domain requires assuming, in the first place, a set of relationships between variables that the data do not show by themselves [@pearl2018, p.34]. Theory supplies the causal models that must guide scientific observation.

As we will show, studies of policies in the Amazon rainforest draw on a varied set of assumptions to select the factors of interest and to shape their explanations. In doing so, they rely on a specific understanding given by theory, whether they make it explicit or not. Making that theory explicit has two consequences. It ensures that we work with falsifiable theories, as @popper2005(1934) requires, since we cannot test what we have not stated. And it is a form of what @bourdieu1991 call epistemological rupture. For these authors, following Bachelard, the scientific object is not given by the phenomenon itself. It is constructed against common-sense prenotions and against the unexamined assumptions that circulate in a field. Making explicit the theory that Amazon deforestation studies use implicitly is a step of this kind because we turn assumptions that pass as descriptions of the phenomenon into propositions that can be examined and tested. @king1994 [p.15] add that a research question should make a specific contribution to an identifiable scholarly literature, increasing our collective capacity to construct verified explanations of some aspect of the world. In their words, "no empirical investigation can be successful without theory to guide its choice of questions" [@king1994, p.28]. In this sense, our investigation is theoretically guided.

The second purpose follows from King, Keohane and Verba's other criterion for a research question: it should be "important in the real world" [-@king1994, p.15], that is, consequential for political, social or economic life, or for understanding and predicting events that harm or benefit many people. The theory that guides our understanding cannot be simply the one the analyst prefers. It must fit the reality under study and answer questions and problems found on the ground. @shapiro2002 makes this argument against what he calls method-driven and theory-driven research, in which scholars deduce problems from their theories and methods rather than from the world, apply their tools to whatever fits them, and produce artificial constructs that make little sense outside the discipline. Our theoretical object, environmental degradation, and our empirical object, Amazon deforestation, must therefore be approached through theories built to explain what leads to success in controlling degradation and how those causes interact with another central concern, the social one. In Shapiro's terms, our investigation is a problem-driven one.

The third purpose concerns the decision to bring new theories into this review and to argue that they fit reality better than established ones. This decision has costs and benefits. A common criterion holds that simpler explanations are preferable when they explain reality well. King, Keohane and Verba express one version of this as leverage: "explaining as much as possible with as little as possible" [-@king1994, p.29]. If a few causal variables accurately explain what at first appears to be a complicated effect, our leverage over the problem is high. Another version is parsimony, which rests on the postulate that "simple theories have higher prior probabilities" [JEFFREYS, 1961, p. 47, cited in @king1994, p.20], a claim close to Occam's razor. King, Keohane and Verba treat parsimony as an assumption about the nature of the world, which is taken to be simple, and for that reason decline to adopt it as a general rule. For the authors, some parts of the world are simple, and others are complex [-@king1994, p.20]. Complexity, in turn, is not a fixed property of the phenomenon. Social science tries to make sense of situations we perceive as more or less complex, and that perception depends on whether we can specify outcomes and explanatory variables coherently. As they put it, "complexity is partly conditional on the state of our theory" [@king1994, p.10]. More observations may help, but they rarely suffice.

Popper's account of testability dissolves part of this tension. For @popper2005(1934), a theory that says more about the world has greater empirical content, prohibits more possible states of affairs, and is for that reason more falsifiable. In his sense, the theory with more content is also the simpler one. What matters is not the number of variables but how much a theory forbids and how exposed it is to refutation. A proposition that engages more closely with how the phenomenon works is more falsifiable and, in this sense, more scientific. @lakatos1978 extends this reasoning from single theories to series of theories, or research programmes, and offers a criterion for preferring a new theory over an established one. A theory T2 supersedes T1 when it accounts for everything T1 explained, has excess empirical content, that is, predicts facts that T1 did not, and has some of that excess content corroborated. A shift of this kind is progressive. A shift that merely accommodates anomalies without adding content is degenerating. This criterion frames the task of the present review. The eco-social state literature is worth bringing into the study of Amazon deforestation only if it accounts for what economic, political-economy, and institutional explanations already account for, and adds testable content about the interdependence between social policy and environmental outcomes. Knowing the established theories and their rivals is what allows us to build explanations that are at once better fitted to reality and no more complex than they need to be.

## Chapter guidances

### The narrative arc

This review is a narrative literature review, an argument, not an annotated bibliography. It is structured by analytical lens, and chronologically within each lens block, so that the first analytical thread and the order of exposition coincide. Three blocks (revised 2026-10-18; the earlier arc was chronological across the whole review):

1.  **The economic lens: income-seeking behavior and its aggregate consequences.** Hardin and Olson (the tragedy of the commons; collective action and the free rider), then environmental economics and the Environmental Kuznets Curve [@hardin1968; @olson2003; @grossman1994]. Hardin and Olson start from individual behavior facing a collective action problem whose repercussions are aggregate (the free-rider problem); institutions enter *a posteriori*, as the remedy for non-cooperation. The EKC is structural and process-oriented: aggregate growth and the incorporation of technology move the outcome.
2.  **The political-economy lens: the distribution of power and income.** Boyce and the inequality-environment lineage [@boyce1994; @torras1998]. Aggregate outcomes again, through a macro-structural mechanism: the power-weighted social decision rule, by which those who gain from degradation impose its costs on others because of their superior economic and political power.
3.  **The institutional lens: institutions as the causal mechanism.** Ostrom on the governance of common-pool resources [@ostrom2015]; the Environmental State [@duit2016; @meadowcroft2014]; the Eco-Social State (Gough, Koch, Fritz, Zimmermann, Hirvilammi, Bohnenberger). Ostrom keeps the individual as the unit and shows how institutional design makes cooperation possible. The Environmental State literature studies how a country's environmental institutions are created and with what consequences, at the meso level. The Eco-Social State literature adds social policy as an institutional dimension that must be considered to understand environmental outcomes, and the reverse.

Two instructions follow from the ordering. First, the review is no longer chronological across blocks (Ostrom, 1990, comes after Boyce, 1994, and the EKC of 1991-1995 comes before both); the draft says once that the order is analytical. Second, *Governing the Commons* is written as a reply to Hardin, and the political-economy block now separates the two. The Hardin passage forward-references the reply, and the Ostrom passage opens with it, so the dialogue is kept.

The context in which each theory emerged (checklist item 6, optional) is told per block, where reliable sources allow.

### Analytical threads

Theories chase answers to the puzzle the introduction poses, and the threads dissect their answers. Each thread is a question asked of every theory; the answers are what the defense section below works with. Three threads (revised 2026-10-18; the earlier "environmental–social relationship" thread is now the well-being question inside the lens thread):

- **Lens (economic, political economy, institutional).** Question: how does the theory approach the environmental puzzle, and what does it take well-being to be? The lens names the dimension of social interaction that carries the explanation: rent and growth (economic), inequality and distribution (political economy), institutions and governance (institutional). It also fixes, in most cases, the theory's conception of well-being: income for the economic lens, distribution for political economy. Inside the institutional lens the conception varies, and the review records it theory by theory: Ostrom reads well-being as the livelihood appropriators draw from the resource; the Environmental State literature barely addresses it; the Eco-Social State reads it as human needs met by institutions (Doyal and Gough 1991; Gough 2017, to add in Zotero) and is the only approach that brings well-being institutions into the explanation. That within-lens variation is what the defense section relies on. The context of theory emergence (checklist item 6) belongs to this thread.
- **Level and range (micro, meso, macro).** Question: what is the unit of analysis, and what is the scope of the argument? Two senses are kept apart. Level is the unit: individual, arrangement, aggregate. Range is the scope: general mechanisms that hold across contexts, or middle-range explanations contingent on institutional arrangements. Hardin, Olson and the EKC offer macro arguments; Hardin and Olson begin with the individual, but the consequences are aggregate (micro-to-macro). The political economy of the environment is also micro-to-macro, since it explains aggregate outcomes of power-weighted behavior. Ostrom, the Environmental State and the Eco-Social State offer middle-range explanations: they center institutional arrangements that vary by context, so their claims are contingent, not structural. Ostrom is micro-to-meso. The Environmental State literature is meso in the range sense although it studies national states: the arrangement explains the outcome and varies across cases. Only the institutional approaches treat variation across governing units as evidence for the explanation, and only they can therefore differentiate levels of government and conceive a multilevel perspective. Political economy can be tested across units [@boyce1999], but there the units are observations, not explanations.
- **Locus of institutions.** Question: what role do institutions play in the mechanism? Every theory includes rules; they differ on where the rules sit. Hardin's world starts without institutions, an unowned resource and no rules, and institutions are created afterwards to solve the collective action problem, top-down. Ostrom's world starts with institutions, and variation in their design produces different collective-action outcomes; institutions are the basis of cooperation and are built bottom-up. In the institutional approaches generally, institutions are explanatory: they cause the outcomes, and because configurations vary, outcomes vary. Only the Eco-Social State brings social institutions into the explanatory mechanism of the environmental puzzle.

### The defense of the eco-social perspective

The review closes its three blocks by defending the eco-social perspective as the one that best captures the dimensions at stake in the environmental puzzle. The section sits between the narrative review and the gap section, and it is what makes the move to the gaps coherent (revision instructions, step 1, below). It makes two moves.

#### Why the eco-social perspective

Four claims, each resting on a thread:

- It considers distribution, the dimension the political-economy lens made central (lens thread). The eco-social literature treats inequality as a constraint on and an outcome of eco-social policy [@gough2016; @koch2014; @buchs2021], but it does not carry political economy's mechanism of power-weighted decision. That mechanism is what the second move borrows.
- It considers the causal role of institutions, drawing on the Environmental State literature and on neoinstitutionalism (locus thread).
- It is the only approach that brings well-being institutions into the explanation of environmental governance and outcomes (lens thread, the well-being question).
- Its meso-level analysis permits comparison across units of government and a multilevel perspective (level and range thread).

If the revised epistemological section keeps the Lakatosian criterion, the four claims are its application: the eco-social perspective accounts for what the other traditions account for (distribution, the causal role of institutions) and adds content they lack (well-being institutions, the meso range). If the criterion is cut there, the four claims stand on the threads alone.

#### What it borrows from political economy: the asset typology

The eco-social perspective says that well-being institutions matter for environmental outcomes. It does not say who gains and who loses when environmental action is taken, which is political economy's question. The chapter closes that gap with a construction of the author's, drawing on the asset-revaluation argument of Colgan, Green and Hale (2021; to add in Zotero), itself in the political-economy lineage (Aklin and Mildenberger 2020, distributive conflict rather than collective action; to add in Zotero). In that argument, assets are all inputs to production and sources of material wealth: capital, labor and natural endowments. Climate politics is a contest between holders of climate-forcing assets (oil fields, cattle farms) and holders of climate-vulnerable assets (coastal property, fisheries). Power is a function of holdings, instrumental and structural. The contest becomes existential when the asset is central to the holder and faces total elimination rather than marginal loss. Labor is an asset in this model: the coal miner who cannot retrain holds a forcing asset with no exit, and a whole town can depend on one asset indirectly.

The chapter transposes the model to deforestation and adds a second axis. The paper collapses capital and labor holders of forcing assets into one class; the chapter splits them by what the holder stands to lose, because the two respond differently to policy and because the split is where social policy enters.

| | Degradation-forcing assets (gain from degradation) | Conservation-aligned assets (gain from conservation) |
|---|---|---|
| **Rent assets** (capital; loss is marginal, the holder is diversified) | Ranchers, land speculators, soy capital | Bioeconomy capital, carbon-credit and tourism assets |
| **Livelihood assets** (labor and the well-being that depends on it; loss is existential) | Workers in degradation-linked activities, their households, and the local commerce and services that live from them; smallholders, garimpeiros | Indigenous peoples, extractivists, ribeirinhos |

The row distinction uses the paper's own existential criterion: a livelihood asset is one whose loss is existential for the holder, a rent asset one whose loss is marginal. Dependence in the livelihood row is direct or indirect: it covers the whole population whose well-being rests on the results of the degrading activity, employed in it or not. Actors hold mixes, and the typology places them on a spectrum rather than in boxes. The vocabulary keeps the paper's axis (assets that force degradation versus assets aligned with conservation) with two departures in naming. The conservation side is not called "dependent", because in this dissertation dependence names the livelihood relation, which is the second axis. The livelihood cell is not called "vulnerable", because in the source "vulnerable" names an asset's exposure to climate change, not the holder's social condition.

Three things follow. First, the typology is the distributive content of the framework: operationalizable dimension 1 below asks who holds which asset, in which form. Second, the degradation-forcing livelihood cell is the hinge to dimension 2: whether the institutional configuration decouples the holder's well-being from the asset is what social protection can or cannot do, and it is the only cell in which the eco-social perspective adds to political economy. Third, the introduction's dependence argument restates in these terms. Livelihood dependence is holding a degradation-forcing livelihood asset with no exit. Institutional dependence is the state itself holding degradation-forcing assets, as the paper says of Saudi Arabia's oil and as holds for Amazon states whose budgets rest on ICMS from agriculture and cattle, but not for municipalities whose budgets rest on federal transfers. The fiscal reading of the two arenas (Chapter 2) follows from the typology. For Chapter 4, the share of local employment and output in degradation-linked activities is the municipal proxy for the livelihood row.

The paper reads regulatory politics through the concentration and dispersion of costs and benefits (Wilson 1980; to add in Zotero). That reading gives Chapter 2's two-arenas contrast a source: enforcement on the frontier has concentrated local costs and dispersed benefits, the configuration in which a rule finds no local defender.

**Sources to add in Zotero before drafting this section:** Colgan, Green and Hale 2021 (*International Organization* 75(2): 586-610); Aklin and Mildenberger 2020 (*Global Environmental Politics* 20(4): 4-27); Wilson 1980 (*The Politics of Regulation*); Doyal and Gough 1991 and Gough 2017 (the needs conception of well-being, shared with the introduction's list).

#### Current description of the argument

The Eco-Social State literature rests on two assumptions that do not hold in frontier settings: a consolidated welfare state and a state that enforces the environmental rules it adopts. Latin American scholarship on welfare expansion and on institutional weakness theorizes precisely the conditions under which these assumptions fail, and the chapter draws on it to extend the framework. The first body of work explains the recent incorporation of "outsiders," the population outside formal employment and therefore outside contributory social insurance. It attributes this incorporation to redemocratization, electoral competition, left-wing parties, and social movement pressure [@arretche2018; @garay2016]. The second body of work shows that non-enforcement of regulations, including environmental ones, is often a political choice rather than a capacity deficit. Politicians may deliberately forbear from enforcing rules whose application would impose costs on poor constituents, using non-enforcement as a form of informal redistribution [@holland2016]. Enforcement of forest protection, in turn, varies subnationally with the balance of power between landowner coalitions and social or environmental movements, and with the alignment between national and subnational governments [@milmanda2019; @milmanda2020; @milmanda2020a].

#### The three operationalizable dimensions

Building on these insights, the chapter proposes a framework for analyzing the nexus between social and environmental policy in frontier contexts marked by recent welfare expansion and uneven enforcement. The framework has three operationalizable dimensions, all defined around the environment and partitioned by the kind of question each asks (revised 2026-10-18; dimension 2 was "social conditions of state action"). Dimension 1 asks who gains and who pays. Dimension 2 asks what rules and provisions exist. Dimension 3 asks how rules are made and applied across levels of government. The line between 2 and 3 is the line between a rule and its enforcement; the line between 1 and 2 is the line between the distribution of gains and losses and the institutions that produce it.

1.  **Distributive conflict.** Who captures the rents of degradation and who bears the costs of conservation on the frontier, and how the conflict is politically organized. Operationalized through the asset typology: who holds degradation-forcing and conservation-aligned assets, as rent or as livelihood. Captured by the rent structure (land, herd, agricultural output and their concentration), by who is sanctioned and who resists, and by the local income structure.
2.  **Institutional configuration.** What the state does, and does not do, as the determinant of how individuals and communities respond to environmental action: the rules and provisions in place. Captured by market incentives and regulation (credit, fiscal incentives, land tenure), by the labor regime and welfare provision (coverage, non-contributory protection, and the fiscal base that protection rests on), and by nature-conservation measures (protected areas, the Forest Code, embargo rules). This dimension carries the introduction's question of which configuration holds on the frontier. It also holds welfare provision, so the hand-off to Chapter 4 (the configuration, welfare provision included, moderates the local response to enforcement) is unchanged in substance.
3.  **Multilevel governance.** The institutional and political arrangements through which environmental policy is made and enforced across levels of government. Captured by the behavior of political actors (coalitions, alignment between levels, forbearance) and by subnational variation in enforcement.
