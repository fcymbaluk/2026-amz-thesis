## Purpose

The introduction opens the dissertation with its puzzle. Scientific work starts from a puzzle, whether a constructed one or one that the phenomena pose, and this dissertation has two: a theoretical puzzle, in the debate on environmental politics, and an empirical one, in the debate on the Amazon rainforest. The introduction states both, joins them, and then does what a dissertation introduction must do: states the research question, gives the argument in one paragraph, describes the design, names the contribution, and lays out the chapters.

The introduction is written last in substance and first in position. Its claims about what the chapters show are promissory until the chapters exist, and every such claim is revised against the chapter that redeems it.

## The two puzzles

**The theoretical puzzle.** Environmental and climate issues are bound up with economic development and social well-being, and that much is agreed. How environmental concerns interact with the other priorities of a life, income, security, well-being, is not settled. The explanations run from the tragedy of the commons and the free rider to the normative program of a human existence within a safe and just space, and they do not converge. Twentieth-century research on the environment concentrated on one other dimension, economic growth, and asked how far conservation could be reconciled with income. Social well-being entered that research as a cause of degradation (the poverty-environment nexus) or as its victim (the distribution of harms), but not as a policy domain with institutions of its own. Bringing social well-being in as a domain sharpens the puzzle: the cases where conservation and well-being do not align, where livelihoods depend on degrading activities and no alternative is at hand, are the hard cases, and they are the cases the eco-social literature has studied least.

**The institutional layer.** How institutions mediate the interaction of environmental, economic and social problems is the second layer of the theoretical puzzle, and the one that Chapters 1 and 2 answer. In Europe, social institutions were built in step with growth and depend on it, which is why environmental policy there is so often socially regressive. In Brazil and Latin America, social policy followed a different path: regulated citizenship and an authoritarian legacy, then a recent incorporation of outsiders. On the Amazon frontier the configuration is different again: livelihoods came to depend on deforestation-linked activities, but the protective institutions that reached the frontier do not rest on those activities.

**The Amazon puzzle.** How was deforestation reduced between 2004 and 2012 while the region grew and social inclusion expanded, and why did the trend reverse afterwards? The explanations on offer rest on market incentives and on federal command-and-control. Whether other policies and other levels of government had any part is the question the dissertation asks.

**The hinge.** The two puzzles meet at the frontier: it is where the theoretical puzzle takes its hardest form, since well-being and conservation do not align by default, and it is where the institutional configuration that the eco-social literature assumes does not hold. One sentence to that effect belongs in the introduction, because it makes the dissertation one argument rather than two.

## The dependence argument (the introduction's own idea)

This is the idea the author added in October 2026; it is stated here so that the chapters can absorb it, and it is revised as they do.

Two kinds of dependence have to be told apart. *Livelihood dependence*: households and local economies live from an activity that degrades the environment. *Institutional dependence*: the state's protective institutions are financed and legitimated by the growth that the degrading activity feeds. In Europe the two coincide. Welfare is financed by contributions and taxes drawn from a carbon-intensive formal economy, so a policy that restricts the activity threatens both the livelihoods and the welfare state's own base, and resistance runs through the welfare institutions themselves (the literature on growth-dependent welfare states; the social regressivity of carbon pricing). On the Amazon frontier, livelihood dependence is high and institutional dependence is low: the protection that reached the frontier is non-contributory and federally financed (Bolsa Família, BPC, SUS and FUNDEB transfers), and municipal budgets rest on federal transfers rather than on local activity (Chapter 4's descriptive evidence puts own revenue below a tenth of municipal current revenue). The frontier therefore has a structural possibility that Europe lacks: social protection can compensate the costs of enforcement without undermining its own fiscal base, which is Chapter 2's mechanism A. But the same decoupling means protection carries no conservation stake, since the two arenas were never integrated, and forbearance can operate by default, which is mechanism B. Which prevails is Chapter 4's question. The introduction poses it; it does not answer it.

A refinement worth one sentence: institutional dependence varies by level of government. State budgets live on ICMS from agriculture and cattle; municipal budgets live on federal transfers. That is the two-arenas contrast restated in fiscal terms, and it predicts where resistance to enforcement concentrates.

## Register discipline

- The introduction poses the A-versus-B question; it never asserts that inclusion cushions dependence. That verdict belongs to Chapter 4.
- Claims about the Amazon literature ("explanations rest on prices and federal enforcement") are stated as what Chapter 3 shows, and are revised against Chapter 3's corpus.
- Claims about Europe's welfare states are stated as the literature's findings, with named authors.
- Phase 1 of the frontier is not laissez-faire (Chapter 2 guidance, working hypothesis): the state manufactured rents while excluding frontier labor by the design of the citizenship regime. The introduction says the frontier was governed for capital and unprotected for labor, or it uses Chapter 2's own words.
- The introduction uses the terms the chapters settle on, and uses them once each: Eco-Social State (not eco-welfare), incorporation of outsiders, regulated citizenship (cidadania regulada), the two arenas, mechanisms A and B.

## Development notes (2026-10-17, with the author's verdicts)

Assessment of the first draft and what was done with each point. The author's verdicts were given on 2026-10-17; "applied" means the text in `manuscript/introduction.qmd` was changed accordingly, under `docs/writing-rules.md`.

1. **"Total laissez-faire" on the frontier** contradicted Chapter 2's phase 1. Applied: the frontier paragraph now says the state was present for capital (rents through incentives, credit and colonization) while frontier labor stayed outside the citizenship regime and any social policy.
2. **"The inclusion of outsiders cushions or mitigates this dependence"** asserted mechanism A. Applied: posed as the question whether protection cushions the costs of conservation or gives local governments a reason not to impose them.
3. **Attributions.** Applied: the tragedy is Hardin (`@hardin1968`), the free rider is Olson (`@olson2003`), two distinct problems; "a safe and just space" names Rockström et al. 2009 and Raworth 2017 (TODO-cite until added in Zotero).
4. **"The 20th century overlooked social well-being"** was too strong. Applied: the social entered as a cause of degradation (Brundtland 1987) and as its victim (environmental justice, `@boyce1994`, Martinez-Alier 2002), not as a policy domain with institutions, which is Chapter 1's claim.
5. **Yellow Vests** are a consumption-side case, the frontier a production-side one. Applied, with both Northern examples: coal regions and the just-transition debate (production side) and the Yellow Vests described as resistance to a fuel tax on car-dependent households (consumption side), with the difference stated. References for the coal case are the author's choice in Zotero (e.g. Abraham 2017; Stevis and Felli 2015).
6. **Polanyi does not define well-being.** Applied: well-being as the satisfaction of human needs (Doyal and Gough 1991; Gough 2017), social protection as decommodification in Polanyi's sense (`@polanyi1944`), consistent with Chapter 2.
7. **"Conservative model of social policy"** needs its regional source (Filgueira, Barrientos, Mesa-Lago). Deferred by the author: the sentence stays as drafted with a TODO-review comment until Chapter 1 (checklist item 3) settles the term.
8. **The Amazon literature** also includes credit conditionality, supply-chain agreements and protected areas. Applied: two sets of causes (market incentives; federal command-and-control with its further instruments), social policy and subnational governments absent, stated as what Chapter 3 establishes and corrects.
9. **Closing sections** (question, argument, design, contribution, roadmap). Author's decision: not developed now; kept as the plan in "Structure of the chapter", items 7-11, and as a PLAN comment at the end of the qmd.
